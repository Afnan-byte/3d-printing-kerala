01. Introduction to 3D Printing

Understanding the core principles and industrial applications of modern manufacturing.

What is additive manufacturing?

Additive manufacturing refers to the process of fabricating physical 3D objects by extruding material, layer-by-layer, to form the desired shapes. For manufacturers, additive manufacturing typically refers to industrial use of 3D printing for activities such as building tooling and fixtures, prototyping and design validation, and production of low-volume end-use parts.

Additive manufacturing and 3D printing

Within the industry, additive manufacturing will typically always refer to 3D printing. However, 3D printing is technically a subset of additive manufacturing, which includes a few other non-3D printing methods of manufacturing.

While the terms “3D printing” and “additive manufacturing” are frequently used interchangeably, one key distinction should be noted. Additive manufacturing typically carries an industrial connotation, often referring to larger-scale use of 3D printing for manufacturing operations.

This distinction is important to note as the 3D printing market includes many cheaper, smaller-scale 3D printers. These do not satisfy the same purpose as industrial printers: they are typically employed as gimmick factories for novelty items, and are not designed to perform in actual high-value manufacturing applications.

02. Technologies Used

A comprehensive guide to modern 3D printing methods and their industrial advantages.

Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM)

Fused Deposition Modeling (FDM®) or Fused Filament Fabrication (FFF), is one of the most common methods of 3D printing. FDM printers extrude a thermoplastic filament in a series of layers over a build plate to create a three-dimensional object.

Markforged recommends FDM printers for their ability to produce high-strength, lightweight, chemically resistant products with excellent dimensional stability and a high heat tolerance.

Advantages & Disadvantages:

FDM is an inexpensive process, works well with a wide variety of plastics. However, parts may be anisotropic (uneven strength), which can limit industrial use to prototypes and low-strength tools.

Selective Laser Sintering (SLS)

Selective Laser Sintering (SLS) falls under the powder bed fusion category. A high-powered laser selectively sinters particles of thermoplastic polymer powder, fusing them layer by layer to create a solid, functional part.

Versatility & Cost:

An ideal alternative to injection molding. SLS is particularly cost-effective for producing durable, high-quality components in low to mid volumes (typically fewer than 1,000 units).

Stereolithography (SLA)

Stereolithography (SLA) is an advanced process in the vat photopolymerization category. It uses a high-precision UV laser to selectively solidify layers of liquid photopolymer resin.

SLA stands out due to its ability to create highly accurate, isotropic, and watertight prototypes with smooth surfaces and intricate details.

Core Principle:

Ideal for applications demanding fine details and precision. It remains one of the most cost-effective solutions for exceptional surface quality.

Direct Metal Laser Sintering (DMLS)

DMLS (also known as SLM) is a technique where each layer is created by aiming a laser at a metal powder bed. It eliminates the need for physical molds and is ideal for precise, high-resolution parts with complex geometries.

Industrial Advantage:

Recommended for printing industrial items with hollow spaces or challenging angles. DMLS parts have excellent surface quality and near-wrought mechanical properties.

PolyJet™ 3D Printing

PolyJet is an advanced technology that precisely jets and instantly UV-cures thin layers of liquid photopolymer. It can create highly detailed, smooth, multi-material prototypes.

It achieves accuracy by layering resins to form digital materials, bonding layers with UV lamps for a finished model.

04. What is Used for 3D Printing?

Materials come in different forms, depending on the 3D printer technology.

Filament

Filaments for FFF (Fused Filament Fabrication) printers. Thermoplastic strings wound on a spool, melted and extruded layer by layer.

Resin

Resin for SLA (stereolithography) and DLP (direct light processing). Liquid photopolymers that cure under light.

Powders

Powder for fusion technologies like SLS (selective laser sintering). Fine particles fused together by high-powered lasers.

What is a resin 3D printer?

The most common form of resin 3D printer uses SLA technology to create parts. SLA uses UV-curable resin as a raw material.

The resin is poured into a glass-bottomed container, into which a build platform is submerged. A UV laser shines UV light on the resin to selectively harden it into a horizontal layer of the CAD data which forms the part. The platform then raises out of the container, allowing the uncured resin to level out. This process is repeated until a complete object is formed.

What is a powder 3D printer?

The most common form of powder 3D printer uses SLS technology to create parts. SLS uses a powdered raw material, typically a polymer.

The powder is stored in a container, where a recoating blade distributes a thin layer of material onto the build area. A high-powered laser fuses the small particles of material together to form a single horizontal layer. The container then moves a fraction of a millimeter to start a new layer, and a recoating blade swipes across the build area to deposit a new layer of raw material.

05. Types of Filaments

Choosing the right material is crucial for the success of your 3D print. Here is a comprehensive guide to modern 3D printing filaments.

Polylactic Acid (PLA)

A biodegradable thermoplastic derived from renewable resources such as cornstarch or sugarcane. It is the most popular choice due to its ease of use and eco-friendly nature.

Inexpensive Biodegradable No Fumes Easy to Print

Example Uses: Toys, architectural models, decorative prototypes.

Acrylonitrile Butadiene Styrene (ABS)

One of the most common materials used for parts requiring high strength and durability. It offers excellent thermal stability and impact resistance.

Strong Durable Heat Resistant Inexpensive

Example Uses: LEGO bricks, automotive interiors, electronic housings.

Carbon Fiber Filament

A composite material combining carbon fibers and plastic. It is lightweight yet offers superior strength, ideal for aerospace and automotive parts.

Ultra Strong Lightweight Premium Industrial

Example Uses: Drone frames, bike components, high-stress parts.

Nylon Filament

A strong, lightweight, and flexible synthetic polymer. Excellent for mechanical parts like gears and brackets that need high wear resistance.

Flexible Wear Resistant Strong Chemical Resistant

Example Uses: Gears, hinges, bearings, industrial zip ties.

Flex Filament (TPU/TPE)

Designed for high flexibility and elasticity. Most commonly used for rubber seals, gaskets, and toys. It offers excellent impact resistance.

Elastic Durable Rubber-like Impact Proof

Example Uses: Phone cases, shoe soles, rubber gaskets.

HIPS Filament

High-Impact Polystyrene is a strong material often used as support for ABS. It is easy to print and offers good mechanical properties.

Impact Strength Support Material Cheaper Stable

Example Uses: Support structures for ABS, food containers.

PVA Filament

Polyvinyl Alcohol is a water-soluble filament used in dual-extruder printers. It is perfect for building complex structure supports.

Water Soluble Support Material Biodegradable

Example Uses: Soluble supports for complex internal geometries.

PETG Filament

A modified version of PET often used in food packaging. It is reliable, durable, and offers excellent chemical resistance and layer adhesion.

Impact Resistance Food Safe Durable

Example Uses: Water bottles, food-safe containers, durable cases.

TPE Filament

A rubber-like material with elastic properties. Known for flexibility, durability, and strong resistance to wear and tear.

Highly Flexible Abrasion Resistant Eco Friendly

Example Uses: Soft-touch grips, flexible tubes, seals.

PC Filament (Polycarbonate)

Known for superior strength and heat resistance. Frequently used for automotive parts, protective gear, and industrial components.

High Strength Heat Stable Industrial Grade

Example Uses: Protective gear, industrial enclosures, light covers.

The 3D printing process follows several important steps:

3D Design

A model is created using software like CAD tools (Computer-Aided Design). This digital file represents the final object.

Slicing

The 3D model is converted into thin layers. This defines Infill (internal structure), Outer shell (surface), and Supports.

03

File Conversion

The sliced model is converted into a G-code file, giving instructions such as movement path, speed, and temperature.

04

Printing

The printer deposits material layer by layer. Each layer bonds with the previous one, forming the complete object.

05

Post-Processing

After printing, the object may require support removal, smoothing, finishing, or painting.

Real-Life Example: Geared Phone Stand

Imagine you need a custom plastic phone stand with adjustable angles:

  • Design the mechanical gear system
  • Convert the complex gears into a printable G-code
  • Print it in one piece or as modular parts

This allows you to customize the viewing angle, save time, and create a functional mechanical tool at home.

08. Troubleshooting in 3D Printing

Solving common problems for perfect prints

The 45° Rule

Any part of your model that extends more than 45° from vertical will struggle to print cleanly without support.

What to Do:
  • Add supports in your slicer
  • Redesign parts to reduce steep overhangs
  • Use bridging settings for small gaps

First Layer Squash

Around 90% of print failures happen in the first layer. The filament must be "squashed" onto the bed.

Fixes:
  • Proper bed leveling
  • Adjust Z-offset
  • Clean bed (no dust/oil)
  • Use adhesion aids (glue/tape)

Did You Know? (LOM)

Laminated Object Manufacturing (LOM) is a unique 3D printing process using layers of paper, plastic, or metal sheets.

Why It's Interesting:
  • Faster for large models
  • Lower material cost
  • Paper-based prototypes

1. Nozzle Clogging

Symptoms:
  • No filament coming out
  • Clicking/skipping sound
  • Thin/inconsistent layers
Causes:
  • Dust in filament
  • Temp too low
  • Burnt plastic buildup
Fixes:

Quick Fix: Heat +5-10°C and push manually.

Deep Clean: Cold pull or use a cleaning needle.

2. Extruder Jam

Symptoms:
  • Grinding on filament
  • Clicking motor
  • Filament not feeding
Causes:
  • Wrong gear tension
  • Dust on gear teeth
  • Back pressure from clog
Fixes:

Clean gear teeth with a brush. Adjust tension: not too tight (crushing) nor too loose (slipping).

If this persists, check for a nozzle clog!
